<p class="article-dek">Something does not add up on the oil market. Crude has erased the entire spike that followed the outbreak of the US–Iran conflict — WTI is back near $72 — yet transit through the Strait of Hormuz is still running near a standstill. If the supply shock has not been resolved, why has the price collapsed? Our answer, and the one angle we think most of the coverage is missing: look at the currency the barrel is priced in, not the barrel.</p>

<p class="lead">The thesis in one sentence: over most horizons the US dollar, not supply shocks or recessions, is the dominant force setting the price of oil — and with the dollar at a 13-month high and still climbing, the "measuring stick" that prices every barrel has gotten longer, repricing crude <em>down</em> even as the physical supply story stays tight. This note argues the dollar deserves top billing in any bearish oil case right now.</p>

Credit where due: this framing was crystallized by a recent Bravos Research video, *"Something Just Broke in the Financial System…"*<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup> We have taken its core argument, pressure-tested it against the latest market data, and added where we agree, where we would push back, and what would change our mind. It is a genuinely useful lens — and, like any single lens, an incomplete one.

## The puzzle: everything fell at once

Start with the anomaly. Oil dropping should be *good* for risk assets — an energy-cost tailwind. Instead, over recent weeks oil fell and the strongest US megacaps (Nvidia, Meta, Apple) fell *with* it. And it was not just those two. Across the same window, per the video's tally: **oil ~−20%, silver ~−20%, Bitcoin ~−20%, gold ~−10%, aluminum ~−10%.**<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup> Crude is down roughly 40% from its war-driven intraday peak.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup>

When *every* asset class sells off together, the honest instinct is to look for the one thing they share. The popular answer is "recession fear." We think the better answer is the denominator they are all priced in.

## The reframe: the measuring stick can change length

Oil, gold, silver, copper, most stocks, Bitcoin — all are quoted in dollars, and the overwhelming majority of their global transaction volume settles in dollars.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn9">9</a></sup> The dollar is the ruler we use to measure their value. The catch is that this ruler is not fixed: **it can get longer or shorter.** When the dollar strengthens, it takes *fewer* dollars to buy the same barrel — so the dollar price falls even if nothing about supply or demand changed. The move can be pure currency repricing, not a real-economy signal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn9">9</a></sup>

This is why the USD index and a broad commodity basket trade as near-mirror images: dollar up, commodities down, and vice versa. Over long stretches this inverse relationship dominates the tape — arguably more of the time than supply shocks or recessions do.

## The engine behind the dollar: the Fed

If the dollar is the driver, what drives the dollar? Overwhelmingly, expectations for the Federal Reserve — best proxied by real short-term yields. When the market prices the Fed *tighter*, real yields rise, financial conditions tighten, the dollar firms, and anything measured in dollars comes under pressure. When it prices the Fed *easier*, the reverse. The severe drawdowns of the modern era — 2008, 2015, 2020, 2022 — all coincided with dollar strength.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup>

That is exactly the regime shift underway. The Fed funds rate sits at **3.50–3.75%**, held for four straight meetings under new Chair Kevin Warsh, and the market has swung from pricing *cuts* to seriously entertaining *hikes*.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn6">6</a></sup> A stronger-for-longer Fed is a stronger-for-longer dollar — and a headwind for oil that has nothing to do with Hormuz.

## The 6-month tell: what inflation expectations are really saying

Here is where it gets decision-relevant, and where we part ways slightly with the bullish read. The Fed's hand is ultimately forced by inflation — and the cleanest forward signal is inflation *expectations*, which tend to lead official policy by roughly six months. So the whole chain — oil, dollar, Fed, stocks — comes back to one question: is the recent energy shock bleeding into expected inflation?

The two gauges disagree, and the disagreement *is* the story.

Consumer *surveys* spiked — Michigan's 1-year gauge ran to **4.8% in May** before easing to **4.6% in June**, well above the **3.4%** pre-conflict reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn7">7</a></sup> But survey expectations are notoriously gas-pump-sensitive and backward-looking. The *market-based* measure — bond breakevens, the forward signal that actually moves the Fed — has stayed remarkably stable through the energy shock, nothing like the jump that followed Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup> If the bond market is right that this inflation impulse is transitory, the Fed has little reason to keep hiking — which caps how far the dollar runs, and therefore how far oil falls on the currency channel alone.

That is the exact combination in which the currency — not the supply shock — becomes the dominant force on oil.

Two honest qualifiers. These are *live* odds that move by the hour, and prediction-market depth here is modest (crude ~$2.5M, Hormuz ~$4.8M, the Fed market ~$49M). Treat them as a real-money sentiment cross-check — useful precisely because it agrees with the fundamental read — not as proof.

## Our call

If you take one position from this note, take this: **when correlated selling hits oil, gold, silver, and equities at once, your first suspect should be the dollar — and the dollar's next move belongs to the Fed, which belongs to inflation expectations.** Our read —

- **The dollar is the right lens for the oil selloff.** More of crude's recent decline is a currency repricing than a Hormuz-driven supply resolution. On a strong-dollar regime alone, oil drifting toward the EIA's $65–74 Brent path is entirely coherent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup>
- **But respect the two-sided risk.** The dollar is the *marginal* driver, not the *only* one. Hormuz transit is still choked; a genuine supply disruption can overwhelm the currency effect and send oil sharply higher regardless of DXY. This is a probabilistic tilt, not a one-way bet.
- **Watch breakevens, not the gas-pump survey.** If market-based inflation expectations stay anchored, the hiking scare fades, dollar strength tops out, and the pressure on oil (and everything else priced in dollars) eases. If breakevens break higher, the opposite — a stronger dollar and more downside for crude.

**What would change our mind:** a sustained break higher in market breakevens (forcing real Fed hikes and extending the dollar's run), or a hard supply rupture at Hormuz that decouples oil from the currency entirely. Either would demote the dollar from lead actor back to supporting cast.

## The honest caveats

**This is one angle, not the whole board.** Supply, OPEC+ policy, and demand still matter — the dollar is a powerful lens, not a monocausal law. **Correlation is not destiny:** the USD–oil inverse relationship has weakened structurally since the US became a net oil exporter, so the historic tightness may not fully hold. **Survey ≠ market** on inflation expectations, and we lean on the market read — but that is a judgment call. **The drawdown figures are approximate**, drawn from the source video's framing, and returns depend heavily on the exact window. **The prediction-market odds are live and thin** — they move by the hour, some are "touch" rather than "settle" contracts, and modest liquidity can distort them. And **forecasts are forecasts** — the year-end DXY and 2027 Brent numbers are house views that will move.

## The bottom line

The headlines are watching tankers in the Strait of Hormuz. The more powerful variable may be sitting in plain sight: the dollar, and behind it the Fed, and behind the Fed a bond market that — so far — is refusing to panic about inflation. If you only watch the barrel, the oil selloff looks like a mystery. Measure it against the ruler that prices it, and it looks almost ordinary. Watch the measuring stick.
