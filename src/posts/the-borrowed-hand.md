summary: On July 30 the yen touched 163.73, its weakest against the dollar since 1986. Over the next two sessions Tokyo bought roughly ¥13.8 trillion of its own currency, and on the second day the US Treasury bought alongside it, the first joint yen purchase since 1998. By August 10 the yen had given back about 46% of the move. The interesting thing is not that intervention failed. It is what the failure tells you about why the yen is falling in the first place.
tags: macro, currencies, rates

<p class="article-dek">The Fed to Bank of Japan policy gap has halved since 2024. The yen is weaker than it was at the peak of the carry trade. Both statements are true, and together they mean the story everyone has been telling about this currency has stopped being the story.</p>

<p class="lead">Our thesis in one sentence: the yen is no longer trading as a low-yielding funding currency, it is trading as a claim on a fiscal position, and once you make that switch every other fact this month falls into place, including why record intervention keeps buying about a week, why the Bank of Japan's most powerful tool is also its most dangerous one, and why the genuinely important thing that happened on July 31 was not the size of the trade but the identity of the second buyer.</p>

## What happened, in order

On Thursday July 30 the yen reached 163.73 per dollar. That is not a round number anybody was watching, it is simply the weakest the currency has been in the working lives of most people trading it. Tokyo intervened that day on its own, and Bank of Japan current account data implies a single-session operation of roughly ¥8.45 trillion, the largest one-day yen purchase on record.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup>

On Friday July 31 it did it again, roughly ¥5.33 trillion, and this time the New York Fed was in the market alongside it on behalf of the US Treasury.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn2">2</a></sup> The yen closed the week at 157.57. The same afternoon the Bank of Japan left its policy rate at 1.00%, an eight to one decision with board member Hajime Takata voting for 1.25%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn8">8</a></sup>

On Monday August 3, Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent confirmed the joint action publicly. Katayama's statement is short and worth reading closely: the ministry "purchased the Japanese yen in coordination with the U.S. Department of the Treasury," the action was taken "pursuant to the U.S.-Japan Finance Ministers' Joint Statement issued in September 2025," and the two would "not hesitate to conduct further joint intervention."<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn2">2</a></sup> The yen touched 155.20 intraday, a near three-month high, up 1.4% on the announcement and 3.8% across the two sessions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn3">3</a></sup>

By Friday August 7 it was back through 158. On August 10 it was trading around 159.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup>

## The carry trade stopped explaining the carry trade

Here is the fact that should reorganise everyone's model.

The last time the yen traded at these levels was July 3, 2024, when it printed 161.92, then a 38-year low.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn23">23</a></sup> At that moment the Federal Reserve's target range was 5.25% to 5.50% and the Bank of Japan's policy rate was 0% to 0.10%. Call the gap 528 basis points. The explanation wrote itself: you were paid more than five points a year to be short the yen, so people were short the yen.

Today the Fed's range is 3.50% to 3.75%, held on July 29 with three members dissenting in favour of a hike.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn10">10</a></sup> The Bank of Japan is at 1.00%, a 31-year high reached in June.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn8">8</a></sup> The gap is 262 basis points. It has halved.

And the yen is weaker.

The same collapse shows up at the long end, where it matters more for real money. The 10-year Japanese government bond yields 2.78%, up from 2.10% in March and at levels last seen in the 1990s. The 10-year Treasury yields 4.65%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn11">11</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn12">12</a></sup> That is a spread of about 187 basis points, against the 300 to 400 that framed most of the last three years. Japanese 40-year paper has traded above 4% for the first time ever, and the 10-year has yielded more than Chinese government debt for the first time in history.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn13">13</a></sup>

If the yen were a funding currency being priced off the interest differential, this is the configuration in which it rallies. It has done the opposite, all year.

## So what is actually selling it

Rising nominal yields plus a falling currency is a specific signature, and it is not the signature of a carry trade. It is what you observe when a bond market is demanding a higher risk premium rather than pricing a stronger economy. Investors are not being drawn into Japanese assets by a better return, they are being asked to accept a worse price for the same paper.

The proximate cause is fiscal. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi took office on a spending agenda of ¥21.3 trillion, roughly 3.7% of GDP, directed at defence, semiconductors, AI, shipbuilding and cost-of-living support, plus a proposed two-year suspension of the 8% consumption tax on food that would remove around ¥5 trillion, another 0.8% of GDP, from the revenue side.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn14">14</a></sup> A supplementary budget of more than ¥3 trillion has already passed and a further ¥19 billion of reserves was set aside for fuel subsidies, with an explicit promise of no extra net borrowing that the bond market has treated as a statement of intent rather than a constraint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn15">15</a></sup> The government's own estimate of the central and local debt stock for fiscal 2026 was revised up by a percentage point to 187.6% of GDP.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn16">16</a></sup>

Then there is the real rate, which is the number that actually determines whether a currency is cheap to be short. Tokyo core CPI ran at 1.9% in July, and the measure excluding fresh food and fuel, the one the Bank watches for trend, ran at 2.0%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn18">18</a></sup> Against a 1.00% policy rate, the real policy rate is around minus one percent. The Bank of Japan's own July outlook says core inflation is likely to run "clearly above" 2% from the second half of the fiscal year, citing wage pass-through, crude prices and, pointedly, the weak yen itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn8">8</a></sup> A country with a negative real policy rate, an expanding deficit and imported energy inflation is not a country whose currency is expensive to be short, whatever the nominal differential says.

And the flows are less speculative than the commentary suggests. Yes, CFTC data showed large speculators running an $11.3 billion net short as of June 22, the biggest since July 2024.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn19">19</a></sup> But Japanese retail traders, the cohort usually cited as the marginal yen seller, had by June flipped to a net short dollar position of ¥2.79 trillion, about $17.2 billion, more than four times the prior month and the largest bearish dollar bet in a series going back to 2008.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn19">19</a></sup> Households were already positioned for the rally that intervention was supposed to deliver. Meanwhile foreigners bought a net ¥10.2 trillion of Japanese equities in the first 24 weeks of the year, most of it currency-hedged, which means the equity inflow arrives with an offsetting yen sale attached.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn19">19</a></sup> Japan can import capital and export currency pressure at the same time.

That is the uncomfortable point. If the short base were purely speculative, a large enough shock would clear it. Much of this flow is structural, and structural flow does not care what the Ministry of Finance did last Thursday.

Japan spent a record ¥11.73 trillion, about $73 billion, defending the currency across April and May after 160 first gave way. The pair reclaimed 160 within six weeks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn17">17</a></sup> Add the two July sessions and the running total for 2026 is roughly ¥25.5 trillion, on the order of $160 billion, against foreign reserves of about $1.37 trillion.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn20">20</a></sup> Call it 12% of the war chest in four months, for a currency that is weaker now than when the year's first defence began.

This is not evidence that intervention does not work. It is evidence about what intervention does. It sets the price at which a persistent flow clears, for as long as the official bid is present. It does not reduce the flow. When the bid steps back, the same sellers meet the same buyers and the price returns to where the flow says it should be. The half-life is the tell: six weeks in May, five sessions in August. That shortening is the market's estimate of how much conviction is left, and it is falling.

There is one honest counterargument, and it deserves stating. Without the defence, the path from 163.73 would probably have been faster and more disorderly, and disorder in the fourth most traded currency pair is a global financial stability problem rather than a Japanese one. Buying time is a legitimate objective. It is only a failure if you mistake it for buying direction, and the official language, "excessive volatility and disorderly movements," is careful to claim the former.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn2">2</a></sup>

## Why Washington showed up, and why it did so strangely

The size was not the news. The second signature was.

This was the first joint US and Japan yen-buying operation since 1998.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn3">3</a></sup> It was conducted under a bilateral framework agreed in September 2025, in which Treasury and the Ministry of Finance committed to close consultation on exchange rate matters and reaffirmed the standard language about market-determined rates and the harm of disorderly moves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn21">21</a></sup> That framework had been sitting unused for eleven months. It has now been activated, which converts it from a diplomatic formality into a live instrument.

The President's framing was not subtle: "They have a weakening yen, and they wanted a little bit of help. And we're always there for Japan."<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn3">3</a></sup> Bessent's was the same in commercial dress, that America delivers for trusted partners, alongside the commitment not to hesitate to participate again.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup> Jesper Koll of Monex put it bluntly, that the two treasuries have "successfully weaponized the yen."<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn6">6</a></sup>

Set aside whether you like it. The consequence is mechanical. If official currency action is now something an ally can request and receive, then intervention risk stops being a Japanese variable and becomes a bilateral one, and the market's ability to price it drops sharply, because you now have to forecast a relationship rather than a reaction function. Billy Leung's observation is the practical version: investors will carry smaller short yen positions if the intervention threat is coordinated, and some of that funding demand migrates to the euro instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn6">6</a></sup> Currency policy has re-entered the list of things that can move a book, after a decade in which it mostly did not.

The execution, though, was odd, and the criticism came from people who have run these desks. Rather than selling dollars, the New York Fed sold euros out of the Exchange Stabilization Fund to buy the yen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn5">5</a></sup> Edwin Truman, a former Treasury assistant secretary, called it weird on the straightforward ground that selling a third currency is less effective than selling the currency you are trying to move against. Mark Sobel, a Treasury veteran, called American participation unwise. Robin Brooks made the point that matters most: "The last thing you want is to give markets any kind of reason to ask questions."<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn5">5</a></sup> An intervention works partly through the clarity of the signal. Choosing an unusual instrument spends some of that signal on confusion. Estimates of the American contribution run to only $5 billion to $10 billion in any case, which suggests the point of US involvement was always the signature rather than the size.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn5">5</a></sup>

One detail in Katayama's statement went largely unremarked and is worth more than most of the coverage. Japan said it plans to use the Federal Reserve's FIMA repo facility.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn2">2</a></sup> That facility lets a foreign central bank borrow dollars against its Treasury holdings instead of selling them. It is a direct answer to the question everyone asks whenever Japan defends the yen at scale, which is whether the world's largest foreign holder of Treasuries is about to become a forced seller of them. The answer Tokyo has just given is no, it intends to pledge rather than liquidate. That is a meaningful piece of reassurance for the Treasury market, and it was delivered in a subordinate clause.

## The trap

Here is why this is harder than a normal currency defence.

The one policy that would durably support the yen is a faster Bank of Japan tightening cycle. The case is building. The July summary of opinions showed at least three of nine board members arguing the Bank could move faster than its recent pace of roughly two hikes a year, Takata has already voted for 1.25%, and markets price around a 50% chance of a September move and a full hike by year end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn9">9</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn8">8</a></sup> Bank of America expects the yen at 149 by the end of 2026 on exactly this logic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn22">22</a></sup>

But look at what tightening does in a country whose currency is weak for fiscal reasons. Higher policy rates raise the cost of servicing a debt stock approaching 190% of GDP, and they do it into a bond market where the 10-year has already risen 68 basis points since March and the 40-year has made record highs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn12">12</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn13">13</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn16">16</a></sup> Every hike improves the carry and worsens the fiscal arithmetic that is driving the risk premium. The cure and the disease share a transmission channel.

This is the constraint that markets are actually testing, and it is why Governor Ueda's caution is not simply timidity. A central bank defending a currency in an EM-style fiscal repricing does not have a clean lever. It has a trade-off between two prices it cares about, and moving either one moves the other in the wrong direction.

## What would actually change the trend

Strip it back and there are three exits. Only one is currently open.

**The Fed cuts.** This closes the gap from the other side and is historically how yen weakness ends. It is not available. The FOMC held on July 29 with three members dissenting in favour of a hike, not a cut, and Middle East energy prices are pushing the same direction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn10">10</a></sup> Pricing for September has moved toward a hike, not away from it.

**Japan consolidates fiscally.** This addresses the actual driver. It is not available either. The Takaichi government was elected on the opposite programme and the food consumption tax suspension is still live.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn14">14</a></sup>

**The Bank of Japan tightens faster than priced.** This is the only open door, and it is half open. September is roughly a coin flip. A hike that is merely delivered on schedule does nothing, because it is in the price. A hike delivered with language that resets the expected path, from two moves a year to something faster, is the one event this quarter that could change the trend rather than the level.

So the base case is not a yen collapse and it is not a yen recovery. It is a grind with an official bid underneath it, punctuated by sharp squeezes when Tokyo and Washington choose to appear, with the ceiling on any rally set by how quickly the Bank of Japan is willing to move and the floor under any decline set by how much of the reserve stack the Ministry of Finance is willing to spend.

## What we would watch

The Ministry of Finance's official monthly intervention figure at the end of August, which will confirm or correct the daily estimates everyone is currently quoting. The September Bank of Japan meeting, and specifically whether the guidance on pace changes rather than whether the rate does. The September 16 FOMC. The 30-year and 40-year JGB auctions, where the fiscal premium shows up first and most honestly. And 163.73, because the next time it trades there the market will find out whether the joint bid is a policy or a gesture.

Intervention bought a week. The question the week was supposed to buy time to answer has not been answered.

<li><strong>The interest rate story no longer fits.</strong> The Fed to BOJ gap has halved since July 2024, from roughly 528 to 262 basis points, and the yen is weaker than it was then. Whatever is driving this pair, it is not the carry at the margin.</li>
