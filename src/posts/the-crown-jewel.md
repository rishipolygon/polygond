title: "The crown jewel: FIFA priced 20% of the World Cup and unpriced it in four days"
date: 2026-08-05
summary: On July 28 FIFA proposed selling roughly 20% of a new subsidiary holding the commercial rights to the World Cup, at a $20 billion valuation, to a group led by Joshua Kushner's Thrive Capital. Four days later it was dead. The terms were not the problem. This is what the collapse says about outside capital in sport, and why the answer to "is private equity good for the teams" is usually a question about duration and consent instead.
tags: sport, private equity, governance
---

<p class="article-dek">A $20 billion valuation, a top-tier lead investor, JPMorgan running the process and a 53-day deadline to say yes. What FIFA did not have was permission, and permission turned out to be the only line item that mattered.</p>

<p class="lead">Our thesis in one sentence: FIFA's proposal failed not because the price was wrong or the structure was crude, but because it tried to sell a perpetual claim on an asset whose value is produced by parties who were never asked, and in football that consent is not a soft consideration sitting outside the model, it is the model. The same mechanism has now killed or repriced four outside-capital deals in the sport in five years, and the pattern in all four is duration.</p>

## The structure, stated fairly

On July 28, days after the World Cup final in New York, FIFA proposed moving its commercial rights and tournament operations into a new subsidiary called FIFA Forward Enterprise. The entity would hold broadcast, sponsorship, ticketing and licensing rights along with the delivery of FIFA's tournaments, including the World Cup and the Club World Cup. FIFA would keep majority control and sell roughly a fifth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn2">2</a></sup>

Thrive Capital, Joshua Kushner's firm, was to lead through a long-duration vehicle reported as Thrive Eternal. JPMorgan was hired to run the raise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn2">2</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn6">6</a></sup>

The offer to the electorate was direct. Each of FIFA's 211 member associations would receive $20 million if they approved by September 19, with up to $40 million each across the 2027 to 2030 cycle and total distributions pitched in the region of $80 million per association over twelve years.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn3">3</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn5">5</a></sup>

Take the development case seriously for a moment, because it is the strongest thing the proposal had. Football's funding is genuinely lopsided, and $20 million does not mean the same thing to the German federation as it does to the Malawian one. For a large number of associations this was pitches, coaching and academies that no domestic sponsor was ever going to fund.

## The seller was not short of money

That is where the case runs into its own balance sheet. FIFA is heading for roughly $13 billion of revenue in the 2023 to 2026 cycle, with Infantino publicly talking up $15 billion, and plans to reinvest around $11.67 billion, a fifth more than the previous cycle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn12">12</a></sup>

This was a record-revenue seller with no liquidity problem selling a permanent claim on its single best asset. When that happens, the interesting question stops being what is the price and becomes why now, and who is served by the timing. In this case the timing sat between a September approval deadline and a March 2027 presidential election, which is the detail every confederation read first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn5">5</a></sup>

## Consent is part of the capital structure

UEFA's 55 members voted unanimously to boycott every FIFA competition. Concacaf's 41, including the associations that had just co-hosted the tournament, rejected the plan. The AFC opposed it and called for an urgent governance review, noting the proposal could not realistically reach broad consensus.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn5">5</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn6">6</a></sup>

The line that did the work was UEFA's: some things are simply too important to sell, and the World Cup belongs to football.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn5">5</a></sup>

Strip out the sentiment and there is a hard financial point underneath. A boycott is not a protest, it is a supply strike. The $20 billion in that valuation is not owned by FIFA in any operational sense, it is produced by 211 associations sending teams, and the moment a meaningful share of them decline, the asset being sold is a trophy and a logo. Consent is not a reputational overlay on the transaction, it sits inside the capital structure, and a valuation that assumes it without obtaining it is not conservative or aggressive, it is unpriced.

Germany proved the same thing from the opposite direction two years ago. In 2024 the DFL had a signed, structured deal to sell CVC roughly €1 billion for 8% of Bundesliga media and sponsorship rights over 20 years. Supporters threw tennis balls and chocolate coins onto pitches, stopped matches, and kept doing it week after week until the board pulled out unanimously.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn13">13</a></sup> No votes, no board seats, no legal standing, and they still killed it, purely by making the asset unmanageable.

## The reputation problem is a duration problem

Private equity's reputation in sport is not really a story about greed. It is a story about time horizons that do not match the asset, and the terms make that plain.

Read the terms again. La Liga sold 8.25% of audiovisual rights for fifty years. Ligue 1 sold 13% of commercial revenue for fifty years.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn14">14</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn15">15</a></sup> Not five, not ten. Half a century of revenue share, agreed by executives who will hold their jobs for a small fraction of it, on behalf of clubs whose supporters were not consulted and whose successors are bound.

In Spain that produced a real fight. Real Madrid, Barcelona and Athletic Bilbao refused to take part and sued, and Real lost in court.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn14">14</a></sup> The participating clubs were required to put 70% of the proceeds into infrastructure, 15% into transfers and 15% into debt, which is about as disciplined as this structure gets.

France produced the other outcome. After CVC's €1.5 billion arrived, the league's DAZN broadcast contract collapsed, CVC wrote its stake down by roughly 73% to around €200 million, and clubs were told not to count on domestic television income at all. The LFP then built its own direct-to-consumer platform and passed a million subscribers in the first month.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn15">15</a></sup>

<div class="callout">
<span class="callout-tag">The asymmetry to notice</span>
When Ligue 1's revenue fell, the fund took a mark-to-model writedown on an illiquid stake and carried on. The clubs took a hole in an operating budget that pays wages this month. The percentage does not shrink in a bad year, only the base does, so a fixed claim on a variable revenue line transfers the downside to whoever has payroll. That is the part of these deals that is genuinely one-sided, and it has nothing to do with anybody's motives.
</div>

## So, is outside capital good for the teams

The honest answer is that at FIFA level the question was mis-specified, and the mis-specification was doing work.

FIFA's money does not go to teams. It goes to 211 national associations. This proposal was never about whether a club gets a better training ground, it was about who owns the pipe that funds the global game and for how long. Presenting it as development funding blurred a governance sale into a grant programme, and the confederations spotted the difference immediately.

At club and league level, capital is neither good nor bad on its own. It is a claim on future revenue with a required rate of return attached, and three things decide whether it helps.

The first is what is being sold. Financing a stadium against its own cash flows is not the same transaction as selling a permanent slice of league-wide revenue, even when the headline number is similar.

The second is term. A fifty-year revenue share is not an investment, it is a mortgage written against people who cannot yet vote. Roughly 12% of clubs in Europe's top five leagues now have private equity in the ownership somewhere, and the structures that have aged best are the ones with an exit rather than a perpetuity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn16">16</a></sup>

The third is who decides, and this is where the American leagues, for all their faults, have been the more careful buyers of capital. The NFL opened to private equity in 2024 at up to 10% per club, the NBA, MLB and NHL at up to 30%, all of it non-voting, and take-up has been deliberately slow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn17">17</a></sup> Capital in, control out, on a defined horizon. It is a boring structure and that is precisely why it survives contact with supporters.

## What it cost

The plan is gone, but the position it created is not. FIFA's chief operating officer told the AP that staff felt deceived and called it the project of just one man. A senior adviser, Carlos Cordeiro, resigned, calling it a bad deal for football. UEFA described the process as shabby, back room and opaque, then said it had lost confidence in Infantino's leadership. By August 3 UEFA's lawyer was writing that legal proceedings were reasonably anticipated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn8">8</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn9">9</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn10">10</a></sup>

The UK Prime Minister, Andy Burnham, became the first leader of a major country to call for Infantino's resignation, on the grounds that the World Cup is not a product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn7">7</a></sup>

FIFA had briefed that Infantino held letters of support from around 200 of 211 federations. Wales was the first to withdraw its letter and others in Europe followed. Candidacies close on November 18, the vote is in March 2027 in Rabat, and a race that looked uncontested a fortnight ago now has plausible challengers being named out loud.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn5">5</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn10">10</a></sup>

Withdrawing the proposal removed the transaction. It did not remove the information the transaction released, which is that the process could get that far without the owners of the sport hearing about it.

## Why this matters outside football

Because the asset class keeps meeting the same wall. Sports private equity grew from 85 deals worth $6.4 billion in 2020 to 95 worth $12.1 billion by September 2025, and the returns case is real: media rights compounding, scarce supply, franchise values up roughly 17 times in 25 years in the NFL.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn17">17</a></sup> None of that is in dispute here.

What FIFA tested was the limit, and the limit is not regulatory. It is that a sports property is a contract with people who do not appear on the cap table, and their consent has no line item, no maturity and no price. Every deal that has failed in this sport failed at exactly that boundary, and every deal that has closed did so by staying on the financing side of it.

A $20 billion valuation, JPMorgan, and a marquee lead investor got four days. That is the number worth remembering.

<div class="takeaways">
<h3>The five things to remember</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>The terms were not the failure point.</strong> A 20% minority stake with FIFA retaining control is an ordinary structure. It died on process, not price.</li>
<li><strong>Consent sits inside the capital structure.</strong> A boycott by the associations that supply the teams removes the asset being valued, which makes unobtained consent an unpriced liability rather than a soft risk.</li>
<li><strong>Duration is the real objection.</strong> Fifty-year revenue shares and open-ended equity bind successors who were never party to the negotiation, and that is what supporters and clubs consistently reject.</li>
<li><strong>The downside transfers to whoever has payroll.</strong> Ligue 1 shows a fund taking a 73% writedown on paper while clubs took a hole in an operating budget.</li>
<li><strong>FIFA money funds associations, not clubs.</strong> Framing a governance sale as development funding was the weakest part of the pitch and the first thing the confederations attacked.</li>
</ol>
