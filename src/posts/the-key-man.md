<p class="article-dek">The demand for Nvidia's chips did not move this week. The guidance did not move. What moved is the price the market is willing to pay for a company whose strategy, sales relationships and export-licence diplomacy all run through one person, now that the person is the subject of an investigation.</p>

<p class="lead">Our thesis in one sentence: the Nvidia selloff is not a bet that secrets were stolen, which nobody outside a classified briefing can know, it is the market finally putting a price on key-person concentration, and the part of the business most exposed is not the data centre order book, which already assumes zero China, but the political capital that kept the China option alive in the first place.</p>

## What happened, in order

A note on what follows. Almost every claim in this story is an allegation, a piece of anonymously sourced reporting, or a statement by an interested party. Nobody has been charged with anything. Where a fact is contested we say so, and we have tried to keep the personal parts of the story to the minimum needed to explain the market.

**October 2025: the customer.** The New York Times reported that Megaspeed, a Singapore-based company that had become one of Nvidia's largest customers in Southeast Asia, had bought large volumes of Nvidia's most advanced chips and was running them in data centres in Malaysia and Indonesia that appeared to be serving customers in China remotely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn7">7</a></sup> Singapore police opened an investigation, and the US Commerce Department was reported to be examining whether Megaspeed had skirted American export controls.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn7">7</a></sup> Senator Tom Cotton wrote to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick describing Megaspeed as a spin-off of a Chinese state-owned cloud company and asking for an investigation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn8">8</a></sup> Megaspeed denied wrongdoing. Nvidia said it had found no reason to believe its products had been diverted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn8">8</a></sup>

**This autumn: the person.** Reporting syndicated from the Times then said US intelligence agencies had concluded that Alice Huang, a former Megaspeed executive (no relation to Jensen Huang), was gathering information for Beijing, that she had a personal relationship with Nvidia's chief executive, and that President Trump had been briefed on the assessment roughly a year ago.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn14">14</a></sup> The same reporting said officials initially did not believe Jensen Huang knew of her alleged role, and that it was unclear whether that view had since changed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn14">14</a></sup> The evidence behind the assessment has not been made public. Beijing denies the allegations.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn13">13</a></sup>

**Sunday November 8: the admission and the board.** Huang wrote to employees acknowledging that he had been unfaithful to his wife.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn2">2</a></sup> The same day the board announced "an independent investigation in light of recent reporting" that Huang "has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort," to be run by a committee of independent directors with outside counsel. It said it had "no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised," that the inquiry would "examine all available information," and that it "has confidence in Jensen and his leadership team."<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn3">3</a></sup> The statement named no committee members, no law firm and no timetable.

**Monday November 9: the tape and the lawyers.** The stock fell 4.3%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup> A retail investor filed a proposed securities class action in federal court in San Jose against Nvidia and Huang under Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5, with a Section 20(a) control-person claim against Huang. The class period runs from October 9, 2025, to November 8, 2026, and the complaint targets statements describing the Megaspeed relationship as routine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup> President Trump said Huang told him by phone that he "didn't know" he was involved with an alleged Chinese spy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn9">9</a></sup> Representative Brian Mast, who chairs the House Foreign Affairs Committee, said he was prepared to subpoena Huang.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn9">9</a></sup>

**Tuesday and Wednesday.** The stock fell another 5.5% on Tuesday and bounced on Wednesday.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn6">6</a></sup> Morgan Stanley was reported to have dropped Nvidia from its top-pick list.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn6">6</a></sup> Senator Michael Bennet pressed the administration on what his office called its failure to stop Megaspeed's apparent diversion of AI technology to China.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn11">11</a></sup>

## Three things are being priced, and only one of them is knowable

The temptation with a story like this is to treat it as one risk. It is three, with very different information sets, and keeping them apart is most of the analysis.

**One: the espionage question.** Was anything compromised? The honest answer is that nobody trading the stock knows. The board says it has no reason to believe so. The intelligence assessment is classified. Huang's account, relayed by the president, is that he did not know. This is the part that dominates the headlines, and it is the part the market can do the least with. A risk you cannot size is a risk you either ignore or price at a flat discount until something resolves it, and two sessions of roughly 5% each looks a lot more like the flat discount than like a view.

**Two: key-person risk.** This is the one that is actually being repriced, and it was always there. Analysts have pointed out for years that Huang runs Nvidia with an unusually flat structure, roughly 60 direct reports by the most commonly cited count, and no publicly groomed successor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn6">6</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn10">10</a></sup> In a normal week that is a feature: decisions are fast and the strategy is coherent. In a week where the chief executive is the subject of a board investigation and a possible congressional subpoena, it is a single point of failure. The analyst consensus, for what it is worth, is that he stays. The point is not whether he stays. It is that the market has been forced to admit the probability is no longer zero, and a company of this size does not have to move its odds of losing the founder very far for the multiple to give back a few hundred billion dollars.

**Three: the China channel.** This is the subtle one, and the one we think matters most for the next year rather than the next week.

## Why the China exposure is political capital, not revenue

Start with what is already in the numbers. Nvidia's guidance for the current quarter, about $108 billion of revenue, assumes no data centre compute revenue from China at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn12">12</a></sup> Licensed sales of China-specific parts have been a rounding error for several quarters. So the scandal does not cut a forecast. Consensus is already at zero.

What it cuts is the option. China was, as recently as 2025, a market worth something like a fifth of Nvidia's data centre business, and the bull case for a return has always rested on one asset: Huang's personal standing in Washington. He is the person who flies in, meets the president, and argues that selling a cut-down part to Chinese buyers keeps them dependent on the American stack instead of accelerating a domestic replacement. That argument has won licences before. It is not an argument a company makes through its government affairs department. It is an argument a founder makes in person, and it works because the founder is trusted.

A physical commodity trader would recognise the structure immediately. Think of it as a marketing book where the most valuable contracts are not written down anywhere, they live in one marketer's relationships with the counterparties. The book looks fine on a mark-to-market basis right up until that marketer is unavailable, and then the question is not what the contracts are worth but whether anyone else can renew them.

Now look at what this week did to that relationship. The alleged intelligence operative was an executive at the very customer that export-control hawks had already named as the most prominent suspected diversion route for Nvidia's chips into China.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn7">7</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn8">8</a></sup> The White House is reported to have known about the assessment for about a year.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn14">14</a></sup> That makes any future licence decision in Nvidia's favour politically expensive for the administration in a way it was not a month ago, because the opposition can now frame it as a favour to a chief executive with an unresolved counterintelligence question attached. Senators of both parties were already pushing Commerce to suspend licences to China and to intermediary countries.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn11">11</a></sup> They have just been handed their best exhibit.

So the trade is not "China revenue goes to zero." It is already zero. The trade is that the probability of it coming back fell, and the person who was the main driver of that probability now has less capital to spend in exactly the room where it gets spent.

## The disclosure question

The class action is the market's way of asking whether any of this should have been disclosed, and it deserves a careful read rather than a dismissal.

A 10b-5 claim needs, in rough terms, a materially false or misleading statement, an intent to deceive or recklessness, and a loss caused by the truth coming out. The last element is the easy one for the plaintiffs: a stock falling on the day of a board statement is the textbook corrective disclosure. The hard elements are the first two. A chief executive's private life is generally not something a company must disclose. The complaint gets around this by targeting the company's statements about Megaspeed, arguing that calling the relationship routine was misleading if the chief executive had a personal tie to one of its executives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup> Whether a court accepts that framing will turn on facts nobody has yet: what Huang knew, when, and who else at the company knew it. The suit is at the very beginning; no class has been certified and no lead plaintiff appointed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn5">5</a></sup>

We would not trade on the lawsuit. Securities suits follow large single-day drops almost mechanically, and their settlement values are small against a company of this size. The more useful signal is the board's own process. An investigation with named independent directors, a named outside firm and a stated timetable is a board signalling it intends to find an answer. An investigation with none of those is a board buying time. As of today, Nvidia's is the second kind.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn3">3</a></sup>

## What to watch

**The committee.** Who sits on it, which law firm is retained, and whether a timeline is published. Specifics are the cheapest credible signal the board can send, and their absence is the most expensive.

**Washington.** Whether Huang testifies or is subpoenaed, and whether Commerce acts on Megaspeed, which Senator Bennet's office says it has failed to stop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn11">11</a></sup> An entity listing would close a door Nvidia has said was never open. A delay would become its own story.

**Earnings, later this month.** The quarter itself should be uneventful relative to the noise, since China is guided at zero. The tell will be tone: whether management talks about licences and China at all, and whether the board update arrives before or after the call.

**The rebound.** Wednesday's bounce says the market is not yet treating this as a regime change. If the committee stays anonymous and Washington stays loud, we would expect the key-person discount to stop decaying and start persisting. If the board names names and moves quickly, most of it should come back, because nothing about the order book required it to go.

<div class="takeaways">
<h3>The five things to remember</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>The business did not change this week.</strong> Demand, supply and guidance were untouched. What was repriced is governance.</li>
<li><strong>Separate the three risks.</strong> Espionage is unknowable from outside, key-person risk is real and was always there, and the China channel is where the lasting damage is most likely to show up.</li>
<li><strong>China is already at zero in the numbers.</strong> The loss is to the option of it coming back, not to any forecast.</li>
<li><strong>The option ran on one person's political capital.</strong> That capital was spent in Washington, and this week it was marked down in Washington.</li>
<li><strong>Watch the board, not the lawsuit.</strong> A named committee, a named firm and a timetable would tell you far more than the complaint will.</li>
</ol>
