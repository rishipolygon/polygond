<p class="article-dek">Six days separate the two documents. The first, dated July 24, reports a 439% net return for the first half of 2026 and invites clients to add capital into a subscription window opening August 1. The second reports an unaudited monthly loss of 67% and opens with five words most managers never have to write: we let you down this month.</p>

<p class="lead">Our thesis in one sentence: Situational Awareness was not undone by being wrong about AI infrastructure, it was undone by expressing a decade-long capital cycle through instruments that settle in days, and the proof is that the moment the fund stopped selling, the same positions rose 20 to 27% on no news whatsoever. This is the second leverage unwind we have written up in a week. It is not a coincidence, and the two are the same trade at different points on the chain.</p>

## The trade, stated fairly

Leopold Aschenbrenner launched the fund in 2024 with roughly $225 million, on the argument he had set out in his essay the same year: that AI is a physical buildout before it is a software product. The portfolio followed directly from that. Own the compute, the memory and the power. Short the software companies whose moats the technology erodes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup>

The long book was concentrated in exactly the names that thesis implies. Bloom Energy, SanDisk, CoreWeave, IREN and Core Scientific were the five largest disclosed positions in the Q1 filing, together above 76% of a $3.86 billion reported long book across 26 positions, alongside SK Hynix, Micron, Nebius and a set of bitcoin miners converted into data centre operators.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup> The short side included Adobe and other software names.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup>

Note the gap between $3.86 billion of disclosed longs and a fund reported above $20 billion in assets. Most of the real exposure lived where a 13F does not reach: swaps, foreign listings and options.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup>

Through June the trade worked to a degree almost nothing does. Net 439% in six months, better than 1,000% since inception.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn6">6</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn11">11</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn2">2</a></sup>

## The multiplier

Roughly four times gross exposure, financed by Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan and Bank of America.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup>

Sit with the arithmetic for a moment, because it is the whole event. At 4x, a 30% fall in the long book consumes more than the entire equity base before you count anything else. In July the core positions fell between 27% and 54%. The Nasdaq 100 dropped more than 10%. The Kospi lost about a third.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup> We charted that last relationship in [the margin call](/blog/the-margin-call) last week, from the Korean end. The interactive figure in that note lets you move the leverage slider and watch the survivable drawdown collapse. The number it returns for 4x is the reason this fund no longer trades public equities.

Then the second failure, which is the more interesting one. The short book was supposed to be the offset. Instead the market rotated into software, so the shorts rallied while the longs fell. Both sides lost, at the same time, four times over.

The explicit hedges did not save it either. The fund carried roughly $2 billion notional of puts on the semiconductor ETF and $1.6 billion on Nvidia.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup> On paper that is protection. In practice it is a hedge on large-capitalisation semiconductors sitting against a long book of high-beta neoclouds and converted miners. Two different volatility regimes wearing the same sector label. The longs fell straight through it.

This is worth generalising. A hedge that is correlated to your book in calm conditions and decorrelated in stress is not a hedge, it is a fee. The only way to find out which one you own is the day you need it.

## The six days

The sequence matters more than any single decision in it.

On July 24 the letter reports the 439%, describes the selloff as one of the best buying opportunities since early the previous year, and opens an August 1 subscription window.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn7">7</a></sup> That is a manager attempting to solve a financing problem with new equity rather than by selling. It is the correct move if it works.

It did not work. The commitments did not materialise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn7">7</a></sup> Around the same time the prime brokers began marketing the fund's positions, from both sides of the book, to other managers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup> Elsewhere in the industry, hedge funds were deleveraging technology exposure at a historic pace in the sessions immediately before the unwind, which means the natural buyers were already stepping back.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn9">9</a></sup>

On July 30, before the open, the entire public equity portfolio, longs and shorts together, roughly $16 billion, moved to Citadel in a single block trade. Millennium had submitted a competing bid.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn5">5</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn10">10</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn8">8</a></sup>

The letter that followed put the month at minus 67% unaudited, described the block as removing all leverage in order to protect LP capital, and committed to running the public book on a fully paid-for basis going forward.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn3">3</a></sup>

Once your lenders are quoting your book to other managers, you are no longer choosing when to sell. That is the line the fund crossed somewhere between the 24th and the 30th, and it is the only line that ever really matters.

That spread is the liquidation discount made visible, and it is the cleanest statement of the whole episode. The assets were fine. The financing was not.

It also explains the buyer's behaviour. Citadel did not take a $16 billion book, longs and shorts together, at a discount because it thinks AI infrastructure is finished. It took it because it has the capital base to hold those positions on its own timetable rather than a margin clerk's, which is precisely the advantage the seller had given up. Parts of the street have since read the print as evidence that the AI trade found a bottom on the day the last forced seller was cleared.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn10">10</a></sup>

<span class="callout-tag">The number that reframes it</span>
The fund is still up roughly 80% on the year.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn3">3</a></sup> An investor who subscribed on January 1 is well ahead. An investor who subscribed in June is down two thirds. Same manager, same thesis, same positions, and the entire difference in outcome is the date on the subscription document. Where returns are levered, entry timing is not a detail of your experience, it is most of it.

## What survives

The private book was never sold. That leaves roughly $10 billion, of which the largest single item is a reported $5 billion stake in Anthropic, taken in the May Series H that valued the company at $965 billion post-money, alongside positions in the chip designer MatX and the AI data centre operator Fluidstack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn7">7</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#fn2">2</a></sup> The vehicle continues as a private investment firm, and an Anthropic listing has been flagged as the obvious offset to the public market damage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn7">7</a></sup>

There is a structural point buried in that. The private positions survived the drawdown not because they performed better, but because nobody marks them daily and no lender can call them. Illiquidity, the thing investors normally demand a premium for accepting, was the only reason a third of this portfolio still exists. That is worth holding onto the next time a private mark is presented as evidence of stability.

## Why this matters outside one fund

Not because of contagion. A $16 billion book absorbed by a single buyer in one pre-market trade is close to the containment case, and the counterparty was the one best equipped to warehouse it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup>

It matters because the trade was ordinary. Long compute, long memory, long power, short the software incumbents, is not an exotic position. It is the consensus view of anyone who has looked at the buildout seriously, including people who own the physical assets rather than trade the equities. The fund did not lose on the view. It lost on the wrapper.

That is the transferable lesson for anyone financing this cycle. Data centres, turbines, interconnection queues and memory fabs are decade-long capital projects. Public equity exposure to them, at four times gross, against three prime brokers, is a decade-long project with a daily settlement window attached. When the two clocks disagree, the faster one wins, and it does not consult the thesis first.

<div class="takeaways">
<h3>The five things to remember</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>The thesis survived the fund.</strong> The positions rose 20 to 27% the day after the seller was removed. Nothing in those businesses changed.</li>
<li><strong>Both sides can lose at once.</strong> A long infrastructure, short software pairing is one directional bet on a rotation, not a hedged book, and July delivered the rotation in reverse.</li>
<li><strong>Correlated hedges are fees.</strong> Puts on large-cap semis against high-beta neoclouds hedge the label, not the exposure.</li>
<li><strong>Raising equity is the last exit, and it closes fast.</strong> Six days separated a subscription window from a forced block trade.</li>
<li><strong>Illiquidity was the only defence that held.</strong> The private book survived because nobody could call it, which is a fact about the plumbing rather than about the assets.</li>
</ol>
